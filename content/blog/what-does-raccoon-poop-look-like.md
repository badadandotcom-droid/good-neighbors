Raccoon poop is dark and tube-shaped with blunt ends, about 2 cm across, and it often has seeds, berry skins or bits of shell in it. The bigger clue is where it is. Raccoons go in the same spot over and over, so you usually find a pile, not a single dropping. Here's how to tell what you're looking at, and what to do about it.

## Size, shape and colour

Fresh raccoon droppings are roughly the width of a dime or a nickel. They look like short, thick tubes with rounded ends. The colour runs from dark brown to black, and it shifts with whatever the raccoon has been eating.

Raccoons eat almost anything, and plenty of it passes through whole. Look for seeds, berry skins, corn or pieces of nut shell. If you see those, you're very likely looking at a raccoon's.

Not sure what you've got? A clear photo in good light, taken straight down with a coin beside it, tells a lot. Photos are welcome, and sending a few before we come out helps us know what we're walking into.

## Where raccoons leave it

A raccoon picks a toilet spot and keeps using it, and other raccoons often share it. The BC Centre for Disease Control notes that one spot can be used by up to six raccoons, and it lists the kinds of places where these spots turn up near homes:

- On a deck
- In the attic
- Inside a garage or carport that's left open
- On or beside a woodpile
- At the base of a big tree or shrub, or on a fallen log or stump

A pile on the deck doesn't always mean a raccoon is living in the house. It does mean one is spending time on your property, close enough to be looking for a way in. A pile in the attic means one has been inside. And since raccoons keep coming back to a toilet spot, it's likely more than a one-night visit.

## Raccoon or squirrel droppings?

People often find droppings in the attic and aren't sure which animal left them. Once you know what to check, the two look nothing alike.

Squirrel droppings are small, dark pellets about the size of a grain of rice. They're scattered along the routes squirrels run, a few here and a few there.

Raccoon droppings are many times bigger and tend to sit in one heap. If you're finding both kinds, you may have both animals.

## Telling it apart from a dog's

On a deck or a lawn, raccoon poop can look a lot like a small dog's. A few things point to a raccoon:

- It's in a spot your dog can't reach, like a deck railing, the top of a woodpile or a closed-in corner.
- It's in one pile that keeps growing over days or weeks.
- You can see seeds or berry skins in it.
- You've seen other signs, like a tipped green bin, a lifted vent or muddy paw prints on the siding.

## Is it dangerous to touch?

Raccoon droppings can carry the eggs of a roundworm that can make a person very sick if swallowed. Human cases are rare. The BC Centre for Disease Control knew of only two in that province. The eggs take two to four weeks to become infectious, and after that they can last for years where they were left.

So the rule is simple. Don't touch it with bare hands, keep children and pets away from it, and wash your hands after yard work near it. Our post on whether [raccoons in the attic are dangerous](/blog/are-raccoons-in-attics-dangerous) explains the risk and how to handle a small amount safely.

## What to do when you find it

1. Leave it where it is for now and keep kids and pets off that area.
2. Take a photo with a coin or a key beside it for size.
3. Look around the spot for a way in: the nearest vent, the soffit above, the chimney or a gap under the deck.
4. If it's in the attic, assume a raccoon may still be using that space until someone has checked.
5. Don't block any hole you find. A mother may have kits inside.

A small amount on a deck can be cleaned up with care. A pile that has built up in an attic, or one mixed into the insulation, is a bigger job.
