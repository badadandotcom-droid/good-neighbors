A raccoon on your roof at night is often just crossing it. Roofs are part of how raccoons get around a neighbourhood. It becomes your problem when the same raccoon keeps coming back to one spot, or when the sound moves from on top of the roof to inside it. Here's how to tell which one you've got.

## Why raccoons go up there

Raccoons are active at night. The City of Toronto and the City of Mississauga both describe them that way, and both note how well they've settled into city life. A roof suits them. It's high, it's quiet after dark, and no dog can reach it.

A raccoon on your roof is usually doing one of four things.

- Passing through. It climbed up one side and it's on its way down the other, or across to the neighbour's.
- Using it as a toilet. Raccoons pick one place and keep using it, and a flat roof or the low corner above a porch is a common choice. Our post on [what raccoon poop looks like](/blog/what-does-raccoon-poop-look-like) will help you recognize it.
- Looking for a den. It's testing the vents, the soffit corners and the roof edge for something loose.
- Coming home. It already lives in your attic, and what you're hearing is it leaving for the night or getting back.

Late winter adds a fifth. Raccoons mate from about January to March, and New Hampshire's wildlife agency notes that males travel several miles then looking for females. So late winter brings more traffic over the roofs, and they aren't quiet about it.

## How it got up

Raccoons climb almost anything. The City of Toronto points to tree branches that hang over the roof and to old TV antennas and towers. The Wisconsin Humane Society adds downspouts, brick walls and chimneys. A fence that meets the garage, a trellis or a stack of patio furniture by the wall will do as well.

## On the roof, or in the attic?

This is the question that matters, and sound answers most of it.

A raccoon crossing the roof sounds like heavy, slow footsteps that travel. They start at one side, move across and stop. It's over in a minute or two, and it may not happen again for days.

A raccoon living in the attic sounds closer and stays put. The thumping comes from one area of the ceiling, often near an outside wall. You may hear dragging, scratching at wood, or a low chatter. In spring and early summer there may be a high, birdlike crying from the kits. And it keeps a schedule: noise soon after dark as it leaves, and again in the hours before morning when it comes back.

A raccoon working on a way in sounds different again. That's ripping, prying and the scrape of metal or plastic, coming from one spot at the roof edge or around a vent, and it goes on for a while. If you hear that, don't wait for it to finish.

## What to look for in daylight

Walk around the house the next morning and look up. Use binoculars if you have them.

- A roof vent that's cracked, bent or sitting crooked.
- A soffit panel that has dropped or been pushed up at a corner.
- Shingles lifted or torn at the roof edge.
- A dark, greasy smudge where something squeezes through the same gap each night.
- A pile of droppings on the roof, the porch roof or the deck.
- Bits of insulation or plastic on the ground or in the eavestrough.

Clean vents, tight soffits and no droppings point to a raccoon that's passing through. Any one of the others is worth a call. Our post on [how raccoons get in the attic](/blog/how-do-raccoons-get-in-the-attic) goes through the usual openings.

You won't see everything from the ground, especially on a high or steep roof. A clear walk-around is a good sign. It isn't proof.

## A raccoon on the roof in daytime

It isn't automatically sick. The City of Toronto notes that raccoons may be active in daylight during breeding season, and a mother with kits to feed is often out at odd hours. A raccoon in the day that walks steadily and looks alert is usually just busy. A raccoon sitting on your roof in the day, in the same place each time, may be one that lives under it.

A raccoon that's stumbling, falling over or seems not to notice people is a different matter. Keep your distance and keep pets in. In Toronto, call 311.

## What to do tonight

- Stay off the roof. A dark roof is how people get hurt, and a cornered raccoon will defend itself.
- Don't block any hole you find. If it lives in there, or has babies in there, closing the opening makes things worse. Our post on having [sealed an animal in your attic](/blog/sealed-animal-in-attic) explains what happens.
- Write down when you hear it and where. A few nights of notes tell us most of what we need.
- Bring pet food in and lock the green bin. Food in the yard is why it keeps coming back through.

The City of Mississauga suggests bright lights or a motion light, and a talk radio playing outside, for a raccoon that hangs around the yard. Those work sometimes. A raccoon that already has a way into the attic usually puts up with them, and the hole is still there.

## Making the roof harder to reach

Cut back any limbs that reach over the shingles, and have an unused antenna or tower removed. Both come from the City of Toronto's own list. Move anything stacked against the house. You won't keep every raccoon off a roof, and you don't need to. What matters is that there's nothing up there for it to open. Raccoons go for plastic vents and loose soffit first, and our post on [raccoons and roof vents](/blog/raccoons-and-roof-vents) covers the vent side of it.
