Humane wildlife removal means the animal leaves your house alive and unhurt, any babies stay with their mother, and the way it got in is sealed so it can't come back. In practice that usually means a one-way door instead of a trap, a check for babies before anything else, and a proper repair at the end. Any company can put the word "humane" on its website. Here's what it should look like on the job.

## The animal leaves on its own

The core of humane removal is a one-way door. It goes over the hole the animal has been using. The raccoon or squirrel can push its way out to feed, and the door won't let it back in. Nobody has to handle it, chase it through the attic or carry it off in a cage.

The animal stays in the neighbourhood it already knows. Raccoons keep more than one den nearby, so it simply moves to another one. Our post on [how to remove raccoons](/blog/how-to-remove-raccoons) explains how the door works in more detail.

## Babies stay with their mother

The babies are where a removal can go badly wrong. When a mother is trapped and taken away, her young are usually left behind. Toronto Wildlife Centre and the City of Toronto both warn that babies left without their mother starve.

So a humane job starts by checking for young, every time. If there are raccoon kits, the hole stays open until they can leave with their mother, so the family goes out together and she can move them to another den. Baby squirrels go into a reunion box beside the hole so the mother can carry them to a new nest. Our post on [baby raccoons in the attic](/blog/baby-raccoons-in-attic) covers the timing.

## The hole gets sealed

Removing the animal is only half of it. If the hole stays open, the next raccoon or squirrel moves in, and the whole thing starts over. That isn't kind to the next animal either.

A humane job ends with the entry point sealed using material a raccoon can't tear or a squirrel can't chew, and with any damage around it repaired. It also means making sure nothing is still inside before the last hole is closed. An animal sealed into an attic will try to chew or tear its way out, and it can die in there.

## What Ontario law says

Ontario's rules set the floor for anyone removing wildlife from a home, whether that's the homeowner or a company hired to do it:

- Anyone who catches or harasses a wild animal that's damaging property must deal with it humanely.
- A captured animal has to be let go within 1 km of where it was caught, and within 24 hours. The City of Toronto says the same on its trapping page.
- It can only be released on someone else's property with their permission.
- The den of a fur-bearing animal, like a raccoon, can't be destroyed without approval from the province's natural resources ministry. The City's page notes that this rule doesn't cover skunk or fox dens.

The City of Toronto also warns that using poison or body-gripping traps on wildlife can lead to criminal or provincial charges.

## What humane removal isn't

Some things get sold as removal and aren't humane, even when they're legal:

- Trapping the animal and driving it across the city. That breaks Ontario's 1 km rule, and if she has babies, they're left behind.
- Trapping and releasing it at the end of your street, with the hole left open. It's legal, but the City itself points out that trapping doesn't solve the problem, because another animal can use the same way in.
- Sealing the hole without checking who's inside.
- Mothballs, smoke or poison.

The City recommends bright lights, a radio and rags soaked in vinegar to make a raccoon or squirrel want to leave on its own. That's humane, and it works sometimes. But the hole is still there, and animals come back to it.

## How to tell if a company works humanely

Ask them to walk you through the job before they start. A humane company can tell you how they'll check for babies, what goes on the hole and when, how long it stays on, what they'll seal it with, and what happens if they find young. If the plan is "we trap it and take it away," that's your answer.
