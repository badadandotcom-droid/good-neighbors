Most home insurance policies in Ontario don't pay for damage a raccoon or squirrel does to your house, and they don't pay to get the animal out. There are a few exceptions, and they matter, so it's worth knowing where the line usually sits before you call your broker. Every policy is worded a little differently, so treat this as what to ask about, not the answer for yours.

## Why the usual answer is no

Home insurance is built for sudden, accidental events: a fire, a burst pipe, a tree through the roof in a storm. Damage from animals living in the house builds up over weeks or months, and insurers treat it as upkeep.

The Insurance Bureau of Canada has put it plainly for years: a property policy is not a home maintenance policy. The federal Financial Consumer Agency of Canada says the same in its own guide: if damage comes from a lack of maintenance, the policy won't cover it.

Many policies spell the animals out by name. One Canadian insurer's list of what its home policy doesn't cover includes direct damage caused by skunks, raccoons, birds, vermin, rodents, bats and insects. CAA-Québec's insurance advice lists the same group. Squirrels are rodents, so they fall in that line too.

## What usually isn't covered

- The animal getting in: the torn soffit, the chewed fascia, the pulled-up shingles, the ripped vent.
- What it did once inside: flattened or soiled insulation, chewed wood and drywall, droppings.
- Getting the animal out, and closing the way in.
- Cleanup of the attic or crawlspace.

## What might be covered

Here's where it gets more interesting. Several Canadian brokers explain it this way: the chewing itself is excluded, but a sudden event that follows from it may not be.

The common example is a fire. A squirrel chews the plastic off a wire in the attic, the wire shorts, and a fire starts. The animal damage isn't covered, but the fire may be, under the fire part of the policy. A pipe an animal chews that then bursts and floods a room is the other example brokers give.

An Insurance Bureau of Canada spokesperson made the same point in an interview with Rates.ca: the damage the animal caused isn't covered, but the resulting damage would be. Whether that holds for you depends on your wording, so ask.

## The water damage catch

This is the one that surprises people. A raccoon opens the roof, rain gets in, and the ceiling below stains. Is the water damage covered even if the animal damage isn't?

Often not. Per CAA-Québec, the Insurance Bureau of Canada says damage from continuous or repeated leaks is not covered, whether or not you knew about them. A hole that lets in water every time it rains for a season looks like a repeated leak to an adjuster. Our post on [roof leaks from animal damage](/blog/roof-leaks-from-animal-damage) explains how that water moves through the attic.

The lesson is about timing. The sooner the opening is closed after you know about it, the stronger your position, and the less there is to fix.

## Questions to ask your broker

Have your policy in front of you, or ask the broker to read you the exclusions. Then ask:

- Does my policy exclude damage by raccoons, squirrels, birds, bats or "vermin"? What's the exact wording?
- If an animal causes a fire or a burst pipe, is that resulting damage covered?
- Is water damage from an opening in the roof covered, and does it matter how long it was there?
- Is anything covered for a detached garage or shed?
- Would a claim this small affect my premium?

If your insurer gives you a different answer than this post, your insurer is right. It's your policy.

## What to do before you call

Take photos before anything is touched: the hole from outside, the attic from the hatch, any stain on the ceiling. Date them. If an animal caused a fire or a burst pipe, call your insurer first, before the cleanup starts.

Don't wait on the animal while you sort out the insurance. Every week it stays, the attic gets worse, and that part is almost never covered. If there's a chewed wire anywhere in the attic, have an electrician look at it before the attic is closed back up. We work with electricians and can point you to one.

## Why sealing it properly is worth it

Since insurance usually won't help, the job you pay for once is the one that should last. Taking an animal out without closing the gap means paying again when the next raccoon finds it. The City of Toronto's advice on raccoons ends the same way: once they've all left, block the holes and repair the siding.
