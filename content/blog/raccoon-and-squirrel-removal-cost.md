There's no honest single number for raccoon or squirrel removal, because no two houses are the same job. What sets the price is how the animals got in, how many ways in there are, how hard those spots are to reach, whether there are babies, and what they damaged while they were there. The same things move the price for a raccoon and for a squirrel. Below is what each one does to the cost, what a fair quote should cover, and the quotes to be careful with.

## What moves the price

### How many ways in, and where

This is the biggest one. A raccoon that tore open one roof vent is a smaller job than squirrels that chewed through the soffit at three corners. Every gap that gets sealed is time and material, and the Canadian cost guides put entry points near the top of the list.

### How high, and how hard to reach

A bungalow roofline you can reach from a ladder is one thing. A steep roof, a third storey or a gap high on a chimney takes more equipment, more time and more care. Older homes with complicated rooflines often have more places to check, too.

### Whether there are babies

If there are young in the attic, the job takes longer, because they're found and handled before anything is closed. A mother raccoon or squirrel with young in spring and summer, and sometimes later, is a different job from a lone animal in the fall.

### How long they've been there

An animal that moved in last week has done less damage than one that's been there all winter. The longer they stay, the more nesting, droppings and chewing there is to deal with.

### What they damaged

Torn shingles, a chewed fascia board, a crushed roof vent or a pulled soffit panel all need fixing before the house is closed up again. Repairs are often priced on their own, after the removal.

### How many visits it takes

Removal is rarely a single visit. A one-way door has to stay on long enough for every animal to leave before the last gap is closed, and trapping jobs usually take several trips. More animals, or a tricky entry, can add one.

## What a fair quote should include

Ontario's own advice on hiring a wildlife agent is plain: get the house inspected, ask a lot of questions, confirm the company has insurance, ask for references, and get estimates that describe the work in detail. Toronto Wildlife Centre adds that a good company gives you a clear plan and a firm price before it asks you to commit.

A quote worth trusting tells you:

- What animal it is, and how they know.
- Every entry point they found, not just the obvious one.
- Whether there are babies, and how they'll be handled.
- What's included: the removal, the sealing, the repairs, any cleanup.
- What happens if an animal gets back in, in writing.

## Quotes to be careful with

A rough idea on the phone is normal. A firm final price from someone who hasn't looked at the house can leave a lot out.

Be careful with a quote that's only for trapping. Toronto Wildlife Centre warns about companies that take the animal and leave the hole, and the City of Toronto says trapping on its own doesn't solve the problem, because other animals find the same way in. A very low number that leaves out the sealing usually means paying again later.

Ask where the animals go. In Ontario, a wild animal caught alive on your property has to be let go within 24 hours and within a kilometre of where it was caught. The province and the City of Toronto both set this out. A company that offers to drive raccoons or squirrels out to the country is offering something it isn't allowed to do. And a trapped mother leaves her babies behind in the attic.

## Will my home insurance pay for it?

Usually not, though it depends on your policy. The Insurance Bureau of Canada lists damage from rodents and insects as something home insurance doesn't cover and reminds homeowners that a policy isn't a maintenance contract. Canadian brokers say most policies leave out damage from raccoons and squirrels and the cost of removing them. Some may cover damage that follows, like a fire from a chewed wire. Call your broker and ask what yours says.

## Why sealing is worth paying for

Taking the animal out without closing the way in is the expensive way to do this job, because you pay for it again. The province says moving an animal often doesn't solve the problem on its own, and Toronto Wildlife Centre puts it simply: there are no empty spaces in nature. Leave the gap open and the next raccoon or squirrel finds it.

That's why closing the way in is part of the job once the animals are out. Lifetime guarantee on every entry point we seal.
