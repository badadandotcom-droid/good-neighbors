If you closed a hole in your roof or soffit and you now hear scratching, thumping or crying from inside, treat it as urgent. An animal shut inside a house doesn't settle down and wait. It looks for another way out, and the longer it's in there, the worse this gets for the animal and for the house. Here's what's likely going on and what to do about it today.

## It happens a lot

Plenty of careful people do this. You saw the raccoon leave, or the attic went quiet for a few days, so you screwed a board over the gap or filled it with foam. A roofer or a siding crew can do it too, closing a hole during a repair without knowing anything lived behind it.

The trouble is that a quiet attic isn't always an empty one. Raccoons can sleep through days of cold weather. Squirrels are out for much of the day and back before dark. And from early spring into summer, there may be babies in the nest that never leave at all.

Both the province and the City of Toronto warn about this in their advice to homeowners. Ontario's page on small animals says to seal entry points but be careful not to seal animals inside. Toronto's raccoon page tells you to make sure no animal is shut in a sealed area, because it will die there and decompose.

## Two ways it goes wrong

There are two versions of this problem, and they sound different.

The first: the animal is shut inside. You hear it moving more than before, often near the spot you closed. It scratches and chews at the patch, then starts testing other places: the roof edge, a vent, the gable, sometimes the ceiling. The usual result is a second hole, bigger than the first, or an animal that comes down a wall and into a room.

The second: the mother is shut outside and her babies are inside. This is the spring and summer version. You hear high chattering or squealing from the attic, and something heavy working at the roof from outside. A mother raccoon or squirrel will tear at a house to get back to her young. If she can't get in, the babies die in the attic.

Either way, the patch you put on is now the thing causing the damage.

## What to do right now

1. Don't add more screws, foam or mesh. Making the patch stronger makes the animal work harder somewhere else.
2. Listen and write it down. Where is the noise, and is it one heavy animal, quick light running, or high-pitched crying? That tells whoever comes what they're dealing with.
3. If you can reach the patch safely from the ground or from inside, take it off and let the hole stay open. Ontario's advice for a squirrel stuck in a chimney or fan duct follows the same thinking: give it a way out. An open hole for a few more days costs far less than a second hole.
4. Don't go on the roof to do it. Not at night, and not on a wet or frozen roof. If the patch is up there, leave it and call.
5. Don't go into the attic after the animal. A shut-in raccoon is frightened and may be defending babies.
6. Keep pets and children away from the room below, and keep the attic hatch closed.
7. Skip poison and smoke. Poison leaves a dead animal in the house, and Toronto's raccoon page warns that fumes can sicken or kill animals that can't escape, babies most of all.

If the animal has come down into the living space, close the door to that room and open a window or an outside door in it if you can do that safely. Ontario's page gives the same advice for a squirrel: let it leave through the house.

## If it's gone quiet

Noise that stops after a few days isn't always good news. The animal may have found a way out. Or it may have died in the attic or inside a wall, and the first sign of that is usually a smell that builds over the next several days. You can check from outside without climbing. Look for a new hole near the old one, fresh chew marks on the fascia, or bits of shingle and wood on the ground. Aspen Valley Wildlife Sanctuary suggests a simple test for an open hole: push a crumpled paper towel loosely into it. If it gets pushed out, something is still using it.

## How it gets fixed properly

The order matters more than anything else.

First, the animals get a way out. The patch comes off, and a one-way door goes over the opening. It lets them leave and stops them coming back in.

Second, babies are checked for before anything is closed again. Raccoon kits stay with their mother and leave together with her, so the hole stays open until they can. Baby squirrels are lifted out by hand and set in a warm box beside the opening, so the mother can carry them to another nest.

Third, the rest of the roofline gets checked. If the animal was shut in for more than a day, there may be a second opening it started, and that has to be closed too.

Last, the opening is sealed and repaired with wood and metal, not foam. A raccoon pulls foam out by the handful, and a squirrel chews through it.
