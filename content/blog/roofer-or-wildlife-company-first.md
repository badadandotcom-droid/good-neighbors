Call the wildlife company first if there's any chance an animal made the hole or is living behind it. A roofer's job is to close the roof. If something is inside when that happens, the repair fails, and you can end up paying for the same spot twice. Once the animals are out, the fix can go ahead, and on most animal jobs the same visit that gets them out also repairs the damage.

## How to tell an animal is involved

You don't need to be sure. These are enough to make the first call a wildlife call:

- Scratching, thumping or running overhead in the weeks before you noticed the damage.
- Damage at the usual animal spots: a roof vent, a soffit corner, the fascia behind the eavestrough, the rubber collar on a plumbing pipe.
- Material pulled outward or chewed, not blown off. Wind lifts shingles in a patch. A raccoon peels back one corner. A squirrel leaves tooth marks.
- Droppings on the roof or deck, or bits of insulation on the ground below the hole.
- A leak that showed up after a stretch of noise, not after a storm.

If none of that fits, and the damage followed high wind, hail or plain old age, it's a roofing job and a roofer is the right first call.

## What goes wrong when the roof is fixed first

A roofer looks at a hole as a place where water gets in. Closing it is the whole point. Most roofers won't go into the attic to look for a nest, and nobody should expect them to. It isn't their trade.

So one of two things happens. An animal that's inside when the hole is closed tears its way back out, usually somewhere new, and now there are two repairs. Or the mother is outside and her young are inside, and she rips the new work open to reach them. Either way the fresh shingles or the new soffit panel is the first thing to go.

Ontario's advice to homeowners says to close entry points but take care not to seal animals inside. The City of Toronto's raccoon page says the same, and adds that an animal shut in will die there. That advice applies just as much to a paid repair as to a board you screw on yourself.

It isn't only roofers. Eavestrough crews, siding installers and anyone replacing soffit and fascia can close a den without knowing it. If you've got work like that booked and you've heard anything overhead, deal with the noise first.

## What a wildlife company does before anything is closed

The order is what you're paying for.

1. The whole roofline is checked for openings, not just the one you can see. Animals often have more than one way in.
2. The attic is checked for animals and for young. Young raccoons go when their mother goes, so nothing is closed until they're able to travel with her. Baby squirrels are set in a heated box next to the hole, and their mother carries them off to another nest.
3. A one-way door goes over the opening. It opens outward only.
4. Once they're out, the opening is closed with wood and metal an animal can't pull apart or chew through.

Only the last step is roofing, and it's the part that fails if the first three are skipped.

Usually not. ## If you need a new roof anyway

Animals first, then the roof. A new roof laid over an active den lasts until the animal wants out, or back in.

A few things make the two jobs fit together:

- Tell the roofer where the animal got in and what was done there, so the repair isn't torn out and replaced with something weaker.
- Ask for metal roof vents, not plastic, and ask that any steel vent guards go back on. Our post on [raccoons and roof vents](/blog/raccoons-and-roof-vents) explains why.
- Have rotted fascia and roof-edge boards replaced while the old shingles are off. Soft wood is where the next animal starts.
- Keep the soffit vents open. They're part of how the attic breathes, and they shouldn't be covered over to keep animals out.

## If the roofer finds animals partway through

It happens. The old vent comes off and there's a nest under it, or a raccoon is looking back at the crew.

Ask the roofer to stop work on that section and leave the opening as it is. Don't let anyone close it, and don't let anyone lift the babies out and set them on the lawn. Then call. If the roof is open to the weather, say so, because that changes how fast everything needs to happen.
