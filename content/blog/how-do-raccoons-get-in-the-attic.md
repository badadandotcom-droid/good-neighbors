Raccoons get into an attic by tearing open a weak spot on the roof. Usually that's a roof vent, a soffit panel or the corner where the soffit meets the roof. Squirrels do the same thing with their teeth, through a smaller hole. Both of them go for the parts of a house that are already a little loose, thin or soft. Here's where they get in, how they get up there in the first place, and what the repair has to look like so they can't do it again.

## Where raccoons get in

The City of Mississauga's list of the usual spots is a good map of a Toronto roof: chimneys, roof and exhaust vents, and the eaves, soffits and fascia, especially at the corners and wherever two parts of the roof meet. The City of Toronto adds loose shingles and chimneys with no cap.

A raccoon doesn't need much of a gap to start. It has strong hands and uses them to pry. The Wisconsin Humane Society describes raccoons tearing off attic vents and pulling up the rubber flashing around plumbing pipes. A thin aluminum or vinyl soffit panel that's come a little loose is easy work for one.

## Where squirrels get in

A squirrel gets in with its teeth. Their front teeth keep growing all their lives, so gnawing is part of what they do, and wood, plastic, rubber and thin metal all give way to it. The City of Toronto's squirrel page lists the openings they use: open vents or holes in the roof or chimney, loose roof vents, rotten or loose soffits and loose shingles.

They don't need a big hole. The City of Mississauga notes that a squirrel can squeeze through an opening of about an inch. If the gap is smaller than that, a squirrel can often chew it wider. Squirrels also move into holes that a raccoon has already opened.

## How do they get onto the roof?

They climb. The City of Toronto points to branches that hang over the roof, old TV antennas and towers, and brick walls, which squirrels climb easily. Downspouts and vines make a ladder too. A squirrel can jump from a branch to the roof edge, so a tree doesn't have to touch the house to give it a way up.

The City's advice is to trim back branches that hang over the roof and take down an antenna or tower you no longer use. Sources don't agree on an exact distance for the branches, so the simple rule is: the farther back from the roof, the better.

## Why these spots give way

Most entry points are a weak spot before an animal ever finds them. The City of Mississauga's advice is to keep eavestroughs clear so water doesn't damage the wood behind them, replace rotted wood and fasten down loose soffits. The Wisconsin Humane Society lists rotten fascia, soffit and roof boards, often from water or ice damage, among the usual ways in.

On a lot of houses here, the trouble starts at the roof edge. Water sits behind a clogged eavestrough, the fascia board softens, and the soffit beside it loosens. A plastic vent cover weakens after a few winters in the weather. None of it looks like much from the driveway, but it's exactly what an animal is looking for.

## Can I find the hole myself?

Often, from the ground, with a little patience. Walk around the house and look up at:

- Roof vents that are torn, bent or sitting crooked.
- Soffit corners where a panel has dropped or pulled away.
- Fascia boards that look chewed, split or dark with rot.
- Shingles that are lifted or missing near the roof edge.
- The cap or screen on the chimney, or the lack of one.

Not every hole can be seen from the ground, especially on a steep or high roof. Finding one gap doesn't mean it's the only one.

## How the hole should be fixed

The fix has to be stronger than the animal. The City of Toronto's advice for squirrel holes is half-inch hardware cloth or sheet metal, reaching about 15 cm past the edge of the hole on every side. For raccoons it recommends galvanized sheet metal over holes and heavy, rust-proof screening over vents. Toronto and Mississauga both say to cap the chimney, and to check the cap and the flashing around it once a year.

Where wood has rotted, the board itself needs replacing. A patch over soft wood gives an animal something to pull against. Loose soffit gets fastened down properly, with screws or mesh, not left to hang.

## Don't seal it with an animal inside

Every one of these repairs waits until the animals are out. Seal a hole with a raccoon or squirrel still inside and it will chew or tear its way back out, often somewhere new, or die in the attic. Babies make the timing matter more. Squirrels have young in late winter and again in summer, and raccoons in the spring, so for much of the year a mother may have a nest up there. Shut her out and she'll work hard to get back in.

The City of Toronto's advice is to make sure every animal is gone before the hole is closed, and to take extra care in the spring and summer.
