Maybe. A quiet attic can mean the animal has left. It can also mean it's asleep, it's out, or there are babies up there keeping still. You can't tell which from the silence alone. And even when the animal really has gone, the hole it came in by is still open. Here's what quiet usually means, and how to check before anything gets closed.

## Why an attic goes quiet

There are a handful of ordinary reasons, and only one of them is good news.

The weather turned. Raccoons sleep through cold stretches and may not leave the den for days. Squirrels stay in the nest on a bitter day. If the quiet started with a cold snap, the animal is most likely still there. Our posts on [whether raccoons hibernate](/blog/do-raccoons-hibernate) and [squirrels in the attic in winter](/blog/squirrels-in-attic-in-winter) go through what each one does.

You're hearing it at different hours. Squirrels are busy early in the morning and again late in the day. Raccoons move after dark. A change in your own routine, a new work shift or a week away can make a busy attic sound empty.

There are babies. A mother with a new litter spends long stretches lying still with them, and when she goes out to feed, they stay quiet until she's back. Toronto Wildlife Centre describes young raccoons and squirrels waiting quietly in attic nests for their mother to return. The Ontario SPCA says raccoon kits can't leave the den until they're about eight weeks old. So a quiet attic in spring or summer may be the fullest it has ever been.

She moved them. This is the good one. Toronto Wildlife Centre says raccoon mothers commonly move their babies from one den to another through the season, and the Ontario SPCA notes it can take her a few nights to carry them all. If that's what happened, the attic is empty for now.

It died. An animal that was sick, hurt or shut in can die in the attic or inside a wall. The first sign is usually a smell that builds over several days. Our post on [dead animal smell in the attic](/blog/dead-animal-smell-in-attic) explains how to tell.

It was bats. Bats make very little sound people can hear, and in winter a colony may not move at all. Quiet tells you nothing about bats. See [bats in the attic in winter](/blog/bats-in-attic-in-winter).

## If it's gone, why not leave it?

Because the den is still there. The opening that let one animal in is open to the next, and it smells like a place where an animal lived safely. Toronto Wildlife Centre puts it plainly: get rid of one animal and it won't be long before another finds the same spot. A raccoon that moved her kits out in June may well be back in December.

The hole also does what any hole in a roof does. Rain and meltwater get in. Our post on [roof leaks from animal damage](/blog/roof-leaks-from-animal-damage) covers how quickly that turns into wet insulation and stained ceilings.

So quiet doesn't end the job. It changes the question from "how do we get it out?" to "is anything in there before we close it?"

## The paper test

There's a simple check that cities and wildlife groups both recommend. Stuff the opening loosely with crumpled newspaper or paper towel and watch it.

The City of Toronto's squirrel page says to leave loosely crushed newspaper in the hole for 24 to 48 hours. If it hasn't been disturbed, the animals have likely moved on. Humane World for Animals, the group that used to be the Humane Society of the United States, gives it three days of mild weather, and longer when it's very cold, because animals may not come and go in a freeze.

It's a good test with real limits.

- Only do it if you can reach the hole safely from the ground, a deck or a window. Don't go on the roof for it.
- It only works when there's one way in. If there's a second opening you haven't found, the paper stays put and the attic is still in use.
- Don't use it if the animal could be a bat or a bird. Maine's wildlife department warns that they can't push the paper aside and end up shut in.
- In winter it can give a false all-clear. A raccoon that's sleeping through a cold week won't touch the paper.
- It tells you about adults that come and go. It tells you nothing about babies that can't walk yet.

## Other things to look for

From the ground, over a week or so:

- Prints in new snow along the roof edge, the top of the fence or the deck rail.
- New chew marks, bent metal or a fresh smudge at the edge of the opening.
- Droppings added to the same spot on the roof or deck.
- Bits of insulation or nesting on the ground under the hole.

And listen at the hours you normally wouldn't: first light, and the hour after dark. Stand under the spot where you used to hear it.

Finding none of this doesn't prove the attic is empty. It means nothing has shown itself lately.

## What not to do

A few quiet days are not a reason to close the hole. This is how most animals get shut inside. A raccoon or squirrel that wakes up behind a patch tears a new way out, and babies shut in without their mother die there. If you've already closed it and now hear something, read our post on having [sealed an animal in your attic](/blog/sealed-animal-in-attic).

Don't leave it until spring to see what happens, either. By then there may be a litter, and nothing can be closed until they're old enough to leave.
