If you can hear an animal in your attic in Toronto or the GTA, it's usually a squirrel or a raccoon, and you can often tell which one without going up there. Listen for two things: when you hear it, and how heavy it sounds. Squirrels are light and quick, and they keep daytime hours. Raccoons are heavy, and most of their noise comes after dark. Birds and bats are the other two to rule out.

## How to identify the animal in your attic by sound

Scratching alone doesn't tell you much, because every one of these animals scratches. The time and the weight tell you more. Before anyone goes up a ladder, keep a short log on your phone for two or three days:

- the time you hear it
- where it is: over a bedroom, near the chimney, by the bathroom fan
- what it sounds like: running, thumping, scratching, chewing, chirping

Record a minute of it too, if you can. A few days of notes says more than one noise in the middle of the night, and it will point you to one of the animals below.

## Squirrels: light and up early

A squirrel sounds small because it is. You hear quick running, scratching and chewing in short bursts, then quiet. Squirrels are rodents and chew all the time, so steady gnawing is a good clue.

They keep daytime hours. The City of Toronto's guide to the city's mammals says grey squirrels are busiest early and late in the day, and an attic squirrel is loudest when it heads out in the morning and when it comes back in the late afternoon. At night it's in its nest, so you may hear some rustling and not much else.

Fall is a common time for them to move in. Grey squirrels build their winter nests around October, and a warm attic beats a tree. They also have babies twice a year, in late winter and again in summer, so a squirrel you hear in spring or summer may have a litter up there with her. ## What's making noise in my attic at night?

Most often, a raccoon. Raccoons are night animals. They sleep through the day and go out after dark to feed, so the noise comes in the evening and through the night. A raccoon is much bigger than a squirrel, and you can hear the weight: heavy steps, thumping, scratching, and now and then a growl or a chatter.

Light scampering at night can still be a squirrel shifting in its nest, or mice.

Raccoon noise in the daytime often means babies. Raccoons have their young in spring, most often around May, and the babies stay in the den for about two months. While she's nursing, the mother may come and go in daylight to find food, and the babies are loud: chittering, crying, and a chirping that's easy to mistake for birds.

Raccoons don't hibernate, but in the coldest part of winter they slow right down and can stay in the den through a cold stretch. A few quiet nights in January don't mean it's gone. See [raccoons in the attic](/wildlife/raccoons) for how we get them out.

## Chirping in the attic: birds or baby raccoons?

Both chirp, so listen for where it's coming from. Birds like to nest in vents. The bathroom fan, the kitchen exhaust and the dryer vent are favourites, and starlings and house sparrows are the usual ones. If the chirping sits right at one of those vents in the daytime, with fluttering or scratching in the duct, it's most likely birds.

If it comes from deeper in the attic, away from the vents, with heavy movement around it, think baby raccoons. If it's birds, see [birds in vents](/wildlife/birds).

## Bats are the quiet ones

Bats are the hardest to pick out by ear. Most of their calls are pitched too high for people to hear, so an attic with bats in it can seem quiet. The better test is the one the Ontario SPCA suggests: go outside about half an hour before dusk and watch the roofline. Any warm evening with no rain or wind works, from spring into early fall. If you spot them, see [bat removal](/wildlife/bats).

Seeing none doesn't rule bats out. They often stay in on cold or wet nights, and from about October to spring they hibernate, so a watch in the cold months will likely show nothing. Don't wait for spring: call, and we'll check for signs of bats.

## Scratching in the walls, or tiny squeaks

Squirrels nest in the outside walls of a house as well as the attic, and the sound is the same, just lower down. If that's where you hear it, see [something in the walls](/wildlife/something-in-the-walls).

Very quiet squeaking or light scratching at any hour, with nothing heavy behind it, is more likely mice.

## Check the outside of the house

You can learn a lot from the driveway. Walk around the house in daylight and look up at the roofline, using your phone's zoom if you need it:

- the corners where the soffit meets the roof
- roof vents that are chewed, bent or pushed up
- gaps along the fascia board behind the eavestrough
- the top of the chimney

A chewed, round hole points to squirrels. A soffit torn open or a vent pried up points to something bigger. A wood soffit that has gone soft at a corner is an easy way in for either one. Take photos. ## Don't block the hole yourself

It's tempting to nail a board over the hole and be done with it. Don't, until you know nothing is inside. Shut a squirrel or a raccoon in and it will chew or tear its way out somewhere else, or die up there. Shut babies in and you've split them from their mother, and she will work hard to get back to them. The Town of Newmarket's wildlife advice says the same: don't permanently block the entrance until you're sure no animals remain, and never separate a mother raccoon from her young.

Don't go up into the attic to find it, either. A mother raccoon will defend her babies.
