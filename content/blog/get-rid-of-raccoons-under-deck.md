Getting rid of a raccoon under a deck starts with one question: is she raising kits under there? If not, a one-way door gets the raccoon out, and a wire barrier dug into the ground along the base of the deck keeps the next one from moving in. If there are kits, the door waits until they're big enough to follow her. Most of what people try first, like lights, mothballs or a board over the hole, either doesn't last or traps an animal underneath. Here's what works, and why raccoons keep coming back to the same deck.

## Why raccoons pick a deck

The space under a deck, a porch or a shed is dark, dry and quiet, with a roof over it. Toronto Wildlife Centre notes that mother raccoons use these spots to raise their young, and the City of Mississauga lists sheds and decks among the places raccoons den. Food close by makes it even better: a green bin, bird seed, pet food left out, or a lawn full of grubs.

## Signs a raccoon is living under your deck

- Muddy prints on the boards, steps or patio that look like small hands.
- A dug-out gap at one spot along the edge of the deck.
- Holes torn in the lawn where a raccoon has been digging for grubs.
- Tipped bins and scattered garbage.
- Droppings piled up in one spot near the deck.

The City of Toronto suggests a simple test. Sprinkle flour on the ground at the gap and check it the next morning for prints. Skunks use the same spots, so if the prints aren't a raccoon's, see [skunk removal](/wildlife/skunks).

## Lights, a radio and repellents

The City of Toronto and the City of Mississauga both suggest a bright light, a loud talk radio and rags soaked in vinegar to encourage a raccoon to move on. Toronto Wildlife Centre says the three together work better than any one of them alone. It works sometimes. But it can't tell you whether there are kits, and when the raccoon goes, the gap is still there for it to come back to.

Mothballs don't belong under a deck. Health Canada says they're approved only for indoor use in airtight containers, with no registered outdoor use. And no spray or powder closes the gap.

## Don't block the hole yet

The quickest fix people reach for is a board over the gap. The City of Mississauga's advice is plain: don't fill a hole an animal is using. The City of Toronto says to make sure every raccoon has left before closing anything, especially from March to July. Close it with a raccoon still under there and it will dig or tear its way back out, or die under your deck. If there are kits inside, their mother will work hard to get back to them.

## Look for kits before anything else

Raccoon kits in Ontario are usually born in spring, mostly from March to May. They stay in the den for about eight weeks before they're big enough to follow their mother out. Toronto Wildlife Centre's advice is to assume a raccoon could be a mother with young anywhere from January to September. A mother also keeps more than one den nearby and may move her kits from one to another.

To check whether the gap is still in use, Toronto Wildlife Centre suggests stuffing newspaper loosely into the hole and leaving it for three days and nights. If the paper stays put, nothing has been going in or out. A test like this can miss, though. Kits that are too young to move won't push the paper aside.

Fall is the easiest time. Toronto Wildlife Centre and the province both point to autumn, once the year's young are on their own, as the safest time to move a raccoon out. The rest of the year the job can still be done. It just waits on the kits. If you hear chirping under the deck, or you're not sure what's under there, call and we'll check.

## Can I trap it and move it?

Not far. Ontario's rule is that a wild animal caught alive on your property goes free within a day, and within a kilometre of the spot where it was caught. The City of Toronto repeats that rule on its trapping page. One kilometre is a few streets, so the raccoon can walk right back to the same deck. And a mother caught in a trap leaves her kits behind, which both Toronto and Mississauga warn about.

Poison and glue traps are banned for wildlife in Ontario.

## How to get it out and keep it out

The way that lasts has two parts.

First, a one-way door goes over the gap the raccoon is using. It can push its way out and can't push its way back in. Every other gap along the deck is closed first, so the door is the only way out. And it goes on only once every raccoon under there can follow it out.

Second, once they're all out, the base of the deck gets a barrier a raccoon can't dig under. The City of Toronto's advice is galvanized heavy-gauge screen, buried about 20 to 30 cm into the ground and then bent outward another 20 to 30 cm, like an L. The Ontario SPCA describes the same L-shaped screen in a trench 30 to 40 cm deep. A raccoon digging at the edge of the deck hits the bent screen under the soil instead of a way in. A board or lattice that just sits on the surface leaves room to dig underneath.

## Why raccoons keep coming back

Because the way in is still open. The City of Toronto makes the point that if one animal found a way in, others will too. A raccoon that has used your deck knows the spot, a mother who was shut out comes back for her kits, and the next raccoon finds the same gap.

Food nearby keeps the spot worth coming back to. The advice from Toronto and the cities around it is the same: keep bins closed, bring pet food in, clean up bird seed and fallen fruit, and keep the barbecue clean.

## Droppings near the deck

Raccoons often use one spot near a deck or woodpile as a toilet. Don't clean it up with bare hands. Toronto Wildlife Centre and the City of Toronto both advise gloves and a mask, and washing your hands well afterward, because raccoon droppings can carry roundworm eggs.
