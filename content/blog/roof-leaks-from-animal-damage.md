A hole a raccoon or squirrel makes in your roof is also a hole for rain and melting snow. The animal is the part you hear. The water is the part that costs more the longer it's left, because it soaks the insulation, stains the ceiling and starts to rot the wood around the opening. Here's where the water gets in, what it does, and what to do about it before the next storm.

## Where the water gets in

Animals open a roof at the same few places, and each one leaks in its own way.

- Roof vents. A raccoon can pull a plastic vent cover off or tear it open. That leaves a hole in the roof deck pointing straight at the sky.
- The rubber or lead collar around a plumbing pipe. Squirrels gnaw these. Alabama's extension service notes that a squirrel chewing the boot around a vent pipe can cause a leak into the attic. The gap is small, so it often drips for months before anyone sees a stain.
- The roof edge. Where the shingles meet the fascia and the eavestrough, a raccoon lifts shingles and pulls at the board underneath. Water running down the roof then gets behind the fascia instead of into the trough.
- Soffits. A dropped or chewed soffit panel doesn't let much rain in on a calm day. In wind-driven rain and blowing snow, it does.
- Torn shingles. British Columbia's and Nova Scotia's wildlife pages both describe raccoons ripping off shingles and trim boards to get into an attic.

Canada's housing agency, CMHC, tells homeowners hunting for a roof leak to start around anything that goes through the roof: chimneys, plumbing vents and attic vents. Those are the same spots animals work on.

## What the water does once it's inside

First it wets the insulation. Wet insulation packs down and stops holding heat, so the room below gets colder and the furnace runs more.

Then it reaches the wood. Roof boards, rafters and the top of the wall framing can take getting wet now and then if they dry out after. A hole that lets water in every time it rains doesn't give them the chance. Wood that stays damp goes soft and rots, and a rotten roof edge is easier for the next animal to open.

Mould comes with it. CMHC's guidance on mould is that dampness inside a material can be enough for it to grow, and that a musty or earthy smell often means it has started. On wet wood it can show up within days.

Last, it shows up in the house: a brown ring on the ceiling, paint that bubbles, drywall that sags. By the time you see that, the insulation above it has usually been wet for a while.

## Why winter makes it worse

Toronto winters go back and forth across freezing. Snow sits on the roof, melts on a mild afternoon, runs into the opening, and freezes again overnight. Water that freezes in a gap makes the gap bigger. A hole that let in a little rain in October can let in a lot of meltwater in February.

There's a second problem. A hole in the roof edge also lets warm air out and cold air in at the wrong place, and that changes how snow melts on that part of the roof. Ice building up along the eaves near an animal hole is common.

## How to tell an animal hole from an ordinary roof leak

A roofer and a wildlife company look at the same hole and see different things. These point to an animal:

- Edges that are chewed, clawed or bent outward, not worn or cracked.
- Insulation pulled up, flattened or pushed into a pile near the wet spot.
- Droppings, or a nest of leaves and shredded material.
- Noise in the ceiling before the stain showed up.
- Greasy rub marks or hair around the opening.

If it's an animal hole and a roofer patches it without anyone checking the attic, one of two things happens. The animal is shut inside and tears a new way out, or it's shut outside and opens the patch from the other side. Either way you have a second hole.

## What to do before the next rain

Deal with the water inside the house first. Put a bucket or a tarp under the drip, move what can be damaged, and if the ceiling is bulging with water, keep clear of it.

Don't nail or foam the hole shut from outside while an animal may be living in there. If it's spring or summer, there may be babies that can't leave. And please don't go up on a wet or icy roof to check.

From the attic hatch, with a flashlight, you can often see what you need: daylight where there shouldn't be any, dark wet wood, or insulation that looks matted. One caution from Natural Resources Canada: if the insulation looks like loose grey-brown pebbles, it may be vermiculite, and it shouldn't be disturbed.

Then get the animal side and the roof side handled in the right order. The animal comes out, the opening is closed properly, and the wet materials are dealt with.

## What has to be fixed, and what can dry out

Wood that got wet once and is still solid can usually dry. Wood that's soft, crumbling or black needs to be cut out and replaced, or the repair has nothing sound to fasten to.

Insulation is less forgiving. Natural Resources Canada's advice is to remove insulation that has been damaged or contaminated by mould or animals before adding new. Insulation that's wet and also soiled with droppings should come out.

Stained drywall can often be sealed and painted once the leak is stopped and everything above it is dry. Sagging drywall gets replaced.
