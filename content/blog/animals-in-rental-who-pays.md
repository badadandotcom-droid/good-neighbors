In Ontario, getting a raccoon or squirrel out of a rented home, and fixing the hole it used, is usually the landlord's job and the landlord's bill. The tenant's job is to tell the landlord quickly, in writing, and let the work happen. There are a few exceptions, and the steps for when a landlord won't act are worth knowing. This is general information, not legal advice: for your own situation, talk to a legal clinic or the Landlord and Tenant Board.

## What the law asks of the landlord

The Residential Tenancies Act puts the main duty on the landlord. Section 20 says a landlord must keep the building and the rental units in a good state of repair, fit to live in, and up to health, safety, housing and maintenance standards.

Pests are part of that. The Landlord and Tenant Board's own brochure on maintenance says a landlord must take steps to control pests. Community Legal Education Ontario goes a step further: the landlord has to get rid of pests and also try to stop them getting in.

Those guides give cockroaches and mice as examples, not raccoons. Toronto's bylaw is wider. Chapter 629, the City's property standards bylaw, says every property has to be kept free of rodents, vermin, insects and other pests. Squirrels are rodents. A raccoon in the attic is hard to call anything but a pest under that wording.

The hole matters as much as the animal. The City's property standards page says that if you rent, your landlord is responsible for repairing and maintaining the roof. A torn soffit, a ripped vent or a chewed roof edge is part of the building, so closing it is the owner's work too.

Other cities in York, Peel and Durham have their own property standards bylaws, and where a town has none, the province's maintenance standards under the Act apply. Your town's bylaw office can tell you which one covers you.

## When the tenant might pay

The Act holds a tenant responsible for damage they or their guests cause on purpose or through carelessness. Normal living doesn't count.

So if a tenant leaves food out on a balcony every night, or props open a garage door that lets a raccoon in, a landlord may argue the tenant is responsible for part of it. An animal that tears its way in through the roof is a different thing. That's the building, not the tenant.

A lease can put some upkeep on a tenant, but it can't take away rights the Act gives. A term that goes against the Act isn't enforced by the Board.

## What a tenant should do

1. Tell the landlord the same day you hear it, and put it in writing. A text or email is fine. Say what you heard, where, and when. Keep a copy.
2. Take photos of anything you can see without climbing: a torn vent from the yard, droppings on a balcony, a stain on the ceiling.
3. Don't seal anything yourself, and don't set traps. If an animal is shut inside with its babies, the damage gets worse, and it's the owner's building to work on.
4. Let the landlord or the company they hire in to do the work. The Act lets a landlord enter to do repairs with 24 hours' written notice.
5. If the noise is in a shared wall or the floor above, tell the landlord that too. In a duplex or a house split into units, the animal is often in a space no tenant can reach.

## If the landlord won't fix it

Follow up in writing and give a fair deadline. If nothing happens, you have two routes, and you can use both.

In Toronto, call 311 or file a property standards complaint online. A bylaw officer can inspect and order the owner to fix the problem. Other towns have their own bylaw office.

You can also file a Tenant Application about Maintenance, Form T6, with the Landlord and Tenant Board. The Board can order the landlord to do the work and can lower the rent until it's done. Steps to Justice, run by Community Legal Education Ontario, has a guided version of the form. Keep your texts, emails, photos and dates. The Board will want to see them.

## For landlords

Once a tenant reports noise in the ceiling or walls, the clock is running. A raccoon or squirrel left in a rental for weeks means a bigger repair and a tenant with a case at the Board. In a house with tenants on more than one floor, one animal can lead to complaints from every unit.

Two things keep the cost down. Move fast, and close the way in properly once the animal is out, so you're not paying for the same hole next spring.
