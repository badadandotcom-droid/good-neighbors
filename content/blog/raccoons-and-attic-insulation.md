A raccoon in the attic flattens the insulation, soils part of it and sometimes tears it up for a nest. Some of that insulation can stay. The parts with droppings or urine in them, and anything that got wet, usually have to come out. Here's how to tell the difference, and the order the work should happen in.

## What a raccoon does up there

A grown raccoon is heavy, and it walks the same routes every time: from the hole to where it sleeps, and from there to the corner it uses as a toilet. Loose insulation packs down under that weight into trails and a flat bed, usually near the eaves or beside the chimney where it's warmest.

A mother with kits does more. She pulls insulation into a pile for the nest, and the kits spend weeks on it.

Then there's the droppings. Raccoons tend to go in one spot, so the mess is often in one or two piles, not spread across the whole attic. Our post on [what raccoon poop looks like](/blog/what-does-raccoon-poop-look-like) has the details for telling it from other animals.

## What squirrels do

Squirrels are lighter, so they don't flatten much. They tunnel through insulation, shred it for nests and leave small droppings scattered around, not in a pile. The bigger worry with squirrels is what's under the insulation: they chew, and wiring runs through there. That's covered in [squirrels chewing wires in the attic](/blog/squirrels-chewing-wires-in-attic).

## Why flattened insulation matters

Insulation works by trapping air. Pack it down and there's less air in it, so it holds less heat. CMHC's guide on insulating a house says the same thing about installation: compressing insulation reduces its real R value.

In practice that means a cold patch on the ceiling below, more heat lost through the roof, and in a Toronto winter, a better chance of ice along the eaves above that spot.

## What can stay and what has to go

Natural Resources Canada's guide, Keeping the Heat In, gives the starting rule: check the insulation's condition, and if it has been damaged or contaminated by mould or vermin, remove it. The same guide says not to cover wet insulation until the source of the moisture is fixed and it has dried.

From there it sorts out like this.

- Flattened but clean and dry. A trail or a packed-down bed with no droppings in it can often stay. New insulation goes on top to bring the depth back.
- Soiled with droppings or urine. This comes out. It can't be cleaned in place.
- Wet. If rain or snow came in through the hole, that insulation comes out too, or stays uncovered until it's fully dry and the hole is closed.
- Torn up for a nest. The nest and what's under it come out.

In most attics the damage is in one area, so the job is a section, not the whole attic. A raccoon that's been up there for a season or more is the exception.

## The health side, kept in proportion

Raccoon droppings can carry roundworm eggs. Infection in people is rare, but it can be serious, and young children are most at risk because they put their hands in their mouths.

Two things from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention are worth knowing. First, most chemicals don't kill the eggs. That's why soiled insulation is taken out and bagged, not sprayed and left. Second, for cleanup in a confined space like an attic, it recommends wearing an N95 respirator.

We disinfect the surfaces after the droppings and soiled insulation are out. We don't claim that the disinfectant kills roundworm eggs, because the public-health sources say it doesn't. Getting the material out is what does the work.

## One thing to check before anyone touches it

If the insulation looks like loose grey-brown or silvery pebbles, not fluffy fibre, it may be vermiculite. Some older vermiculite contains asbestos. Natural Resources Canada says plainly not to disturb it. If we find it, we stop and tell you, and removal is a job for an asbestos specialist.

## Should you do it yourself?

A small pile of droppings on a deck is one thing. An attic is harder. You're working on your knees between the ceiling joists, in poor light, with dust in the air, carrying bags to a small hatch. One wrong step puts a foot through the ceiling below.

If you do go up to look, don't sweep or vacuum droppings with a household vacuum, and keep children and pets out of the room under the hatch until it's dealt with.

## The order matters

Insulation is the last step, never the first.

1. The animals come out, babies included.
2. The hole they used is sealed and repaired.
3. Droppings, nesting and soiled insulation come out, and the surfaces are disinfected.
4. New insulation goes in.

Put new insulation in before the hole is closed and the next raccoon ruins it. Manitoba's wildlife branch makes a related point in its raccoon fact sheet: the scent a raccoon leaves in an attic can draw other raccoons to it, which is one more reason the old material comes out and the way in gets closed.
