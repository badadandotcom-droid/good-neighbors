A skunk under a deck leaves the same way every time it's done right. You find the gap it uses, you make sure there are no kits under there, you let it walk out on its own, and then you close the ground along the base of the deck so the next skunk can't dig in. The part people skip is the last one. That's why a deck that had a skunk last year often has one again this year.

## Why skunks move in under decks

Skunks dig. Under a deck, a porch or a shed they get a roof, dry soil and a quiet, dark space to sleep through the day. The City of Toronto and the Ontario SPCA both describe them as burrowers that use the spaces under buildings and porches, and Toronto Wildlife Centre points out that a deck is exactly the kind of quiet spot a mother picks to raise her young.

What keeps them there is food close by. Lawn grubs and insects, an open compost, a green bin that doesn't latch, pet food left on the patio. The City of Toronto makes a point worth remembering: take one skunk out without fixing what drew it in, and another one moves in.

## Signs a skunk is living under your deck

- Small, round or cone-shaped holes in the lawn, or patches of sod peeled back, often after rain. That's a skunk digging for grubs.
- A musky smell that hangs around one corner of the deck or one side of the shed, even on days you see nothing.
- A fresh gap or a scraped-out hollow at one spot along the base of the deck.

The lawn holes tell you a skunk is feeding nearby. They don't prove it's living under your deck. To check the gap itself, the City of Toronto and the Ontario SPCA both suggest the same simple test: loosely fill the hole with balled-up newspaper or a little loose soil and look again the next day. If it's been pushed aside, something is using it.

## Check for kits before you do anything

This is the step that matters most. Skunks in Ontario mate in late winter, around February and March, and the kits are usually born in May or June. They stay in the den for roughly six to eight weeks before they start going out with their mother, and most are on their own by late summer.

So from spring through the end of summer, assume a skunk under your deck could be a mother with young. The City of Toronto advises against closing a skunk out between April and September unless the young can already move on their own.

The Ontario SPCA gives one more warning sign. If you've covered the hole and the skunk is pacing and digging hard at it, assume there are young inside and open it again right away.

## Lights, a radio and mothballs

The City of Toronto, the Ontario SPCA and Toronto Wildlife Centre all suggest the same gentle push: a bright light aimed into the space and a radio tuned to a talk station, left on for a few days and nights. A skunk wants it dark and quiet, so a mother may move her family somewhere calmer. It works sometimes. But the gap is still there when she goes, and a skunk that knows the spot, or the next one that finds it, comes back.

Mothballs are a different story. Health Canada says there's no registered outdoor use for them in Canada and warns about poisoning in children and pets. Neither the City of Toronto nor the Ontario SPCA suggests them for skunks.

## Don't close the hole yet

Every source we checked says the same thing. Never seal the gap while a skunk might still be under there. An adult shut in will dig its way out somewhere else or die under the deck. Kits shut in will starve, and their mother will work hard to get back to them. A board nailed over the hole is the most common way this goes wrong.

## Can I trap a skunk and let it go somewhere else?

Not far. Under Ontario's rules, a wild animal you catch alive on your own property has to be let go within 24 hours, within a kilometre of where you caught it, and only on someone else's land if they say yes. The province and the City of Toronto both set this out. A kilometre is a short walk for a skunk, and a mother caught in a trap leaves her kits behind under the deck.

And Ontario doesn't allow poison or glue traps on wildlife at all.

## If you get close to one

Skunks would rather leave than spray, and they usually warn you first. They stamp their front feet, raise the tail and turn their back end toward you. If you see that, back away slowly and give it room.

If your pet gets sprayed, call your vet. If spray gets in someone's eyes, flush them with plenty of clean water and get medical advice if they stay sore.

## Rabies and bites

Skunks can carry rabies. Ontario's own case counts show no rabid skunks reported in 2024 or 2025, but that's a reason to stay calm, not a reason to handle one. If a skunk bites or scratches anyone, wash the wound well with soap and water, see a doctor, and report it to public health. In Toronto, that's Toronto Public Health. Outside the city, call your local public health unit. A skunk that's stumbling, oddly tame or acting strangely is one to keep well away from. In Toronto, report it to 311.

## What time of year to get it out

Late summer and fall, once the year's young are on their own, is usually the easiest time. Skunks don't truly hibernate. They sleep through long cold spells and wake up on mild days, and the Ontario SPCA and Toronto Wildlife Centre both warn that a skunk pushed out in the coldest part of winter may struggle to find another den. In spring and early summer the job waits on the kits.

## How to keep skunks out for good

First, the skunk has to leave on its own. A one-way door goes over the gap it's using, so it can push its way out and can't get back in. It goes on only once any young are old enough to follow their mother out.

Once the space is empty, the base of the deck gets a barrier a skunk can't dig under. A board or lattice sitting on the surface won't stop a digger. The barrier has to go into the ground and bend outward, so a skunk digging at the edge hits wire under the soil instead of finding a way in.

Then take away the reasons to come back. Latch the green bin, bring pet food in, keep compost closed and deal with grubs in the lawn.
